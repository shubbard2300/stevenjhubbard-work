MEND Medical Wear

Founder, Architect & Developer · 2026 – Present

An ecommerce storefront for recovery apparel and connected care — washable hospital gowns, residential nurse scrubs, compression socks, and the AirGuard™ fall-detection collar.

Discovery

I started with the category rather than the product. Every competitor photographs folded fabric on white; nobody photographs the person wearing it, which sells nothing to somebody facing a week in hospital. That audit set three things: the positioning, the two buyer segments — patients and the people caring for them — and the decision to lead acquisition with a free fall-risk assessment instead of a discount code.

Architecture

Static-first, deliberately. Hand-written HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on Vercel, with no bundler and no framework for the storefront proper. A catalogue this size does not need a build step, and skipping one buys near-instant loads and a site that will still build in five years without a dependency archaeology dig. The one genuinely interactive tool runs on hand-written React shims so it could not drag a toolchain onto everything else.

Design

Brand identity, palette, and typography, then the photographic direction — patients and caregivers in real light rather than product flat-lays — and a product-page template that had to carry seven very different SKUs, from a cotton gown to a wearable sensor.

Development

Every line is mine: the storefront, seven product detail pages, and the React fall-risk assessment that scores against WHO fall data with charts and a regional model.

Integrations & APIs

The assessment posts to a serverless lead-capture endpoint. Alongside it, a full technical SEO program — www unification, clean product slugs, an image pipeline, security headers, sitemap and robots.

Delivered

  • Brand positioning, identity, and photographic direction from scratch
  • Seven product detail pages — gowns, scrubs, compression socks, the AirGuard™ collar, MEND Pulse, MEND Oxi, and the MEND app
  • React fall-risk assessment built on WHO fall data, wired to a serverless lead-capture endpoint
  • Technical SEO program and a static-first architecture with no framework lock-in

Steven Hubbard LLC

Independent Consultant & Solutions Architect · Feb 2000 – Present

The consultancy’s own site — what the practice does, who it has done it for, and how an engagement runs.

Discovery

Every engagement opens the same way: current-state assessment. What systems exist, which of them believe they are the source of truth, and where the seams are. Most organizations do not have a technology problem so much as four systems that each believe they own the customer. Finding that seam is the billable part; closing it is the easy part.

Architecture

Target-state design, data modeling, integration strategy, and feasibility review — documented as technical context diagrams and as-is/to-be architecture so business and technical stakeholders align before the build rather than during it.

Design

This site and its apex parent are one design system deployed twice: a drafting-sheet identity, a single type scale, and an accent colour that tracks the visitor’s local clock. Hand-written, no framework, same reasoning I bring to client work.

Development

I stay on the tools through delivery, which is the part that keeps the architecture honest — an architect who does not build ships diagrams that cannot be built. Handover includes solution architecture documentation, integration specifications, and reusable design patterns, so the client’s team can extend the work without me.

Integrations & APIs

Multi-system event-driven integration automating project intake and ticket routing across Workfront, Slack, and Salesforce over REST; ERP and commerce data consolidated into Snowflake as a governed executive reporting layer, replacing spreadsheet reconciliation.

Outcomes

  • Roughly 40% less triage handoff time from the Workfront–Slack–Salesforce integration
  • Spreadsheet reconciliation replaced by a governed Snowflake reporting layer
  • Architecture documentation and reusable patterns handed over for independent maintenance

Eye Appeal Inc.

Senior Architect & Developer · Mar 2025 – Feb 2026

A collectible-grading platform for sports card collectors: an AI-scored reveal game, a proprietary grading metric, and a public registry.

Discovery

Three things had to work together, and each one undermined the others if built carelessly: an AI-powered Rate-to-Reveal game, the proprietary EyeQ+ scoring metric, and a global registry collectors would trust as a system of record. A grading platform lives or dies on whether people believe the number, so the discovery work was mostly about where that trust comes from and what would spend it.

Architecture

Cloud-hosted SvelteKit on Vercel. I architected the API integrations and workflow pipelines connecting internal systems to third-party platforms, each one designed as a reusable pattern rather than a one-off — that is the difference between the second integration taking days and taking weeks.

Design

The hero carries the entire product thesis in two buttons, Blur My Card and Play Rate-To-Reveal: grading as a game you play rather than a report you receive. The registry navigation sitting behind them does the credibility work.

Development

SvelteKit and TypeScript throughout, released to production weekly with zero downtime on two-week Agile iterations, with automated QA and data validation folded into the delivery pipeline rather than bolted on at the end.

Integrations & APIs

AI scoring services, third-party collectibles platforms, and the workflow pipelines carrying data between them and internal systems.

Outcomes

  • Zero-downtime weekly releases on two-week Agile iterations

A San Francisco hospitality brand’s site — menus, booking, and a small storefront.

Discovery

Hospitality has a context problem that most responsive briefs quietly skip: the person reading your menu is holding a drink, the room is dark, and they have about fifteen seconds of patience. Everything downstream was scoped against that situation rather than against a desktop comp.

Design

Booking and menu flows designed mobile-first for that context and desktop second. Design, architecture, and build were all mine, which meant the responsive behaviour was a design decision made up front rather than a development compromise negotiated at the end.

Development

WordPress build with the storefront integrated into the brand site, so ordering and reservations did not hand the visitor off to somebody else’s checkout mid-decision.

Outcomes

  • Mobile-first booking and menu flows
  • Storefront integrated with the brand site

First Presbyterian Church

Architect & WordPress Developer · Contract

A congregation’s WordPress site — services, events, and giving.

Discovery

One constraint shaped every decision here: the people maintaining this site after I left are volunteers, not developers. That rules out most of the interesting answers, and it is a genuinely better brief than it sounds — it forces you to justify complexity to somebody who will never read the documentation.

Architecture

Restructured around what a volunteer can safely edit. Anything clever enough to need me later was the wrong answer, so the clever parts were removed rather than documented.

Development

An accessibility pass across templates and content, and security hardening on a WordPress install that had accumulated the usual decade of plugin debt.

Outcomes

  • Restructured for volunteer maintenance
  • Accessibility pass and security hardening
